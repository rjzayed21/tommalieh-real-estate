## Flat Fee vs. Hourly Billing

The two most common ways real estate attorneys charge for their services are flat fees and hourly billing, and which one applies typically depends on the nature of the matter.

### When Flat Fees Make Sense

Standard residential purchases and sales are the most common example of flat-fee work. Because the scope of a typical closing, contract review, attorney review negotiation, title review, and attendance at closing, is fairly predictable, attorneys can quote a set price upfront. This gives clients cost certainty and avoids the anxiety of watching a clock tick during every phone call or email.

### When Hourly Billing Makes Sense

More complex or unpredictable matters are usually billed hourly instead. This includes real estate litigation, contested title disputes, foreclosure defense, and commercial transactions with heavily negotiated terms. In these situations, the amount of work required is harder to estimate in advance, since a case could resolve quickly or could take months of contested proceedings, and hourly billing reflects that uncertainty more fairly than a flat quote could.

## Typical Price Ranges by Transaction Type

While every firm sets its own pricing, it helps to understand the general categories of cost so you know roughly what to expect before you call around.

### Residential Closings

A standard residential purchase or sale, whether a single-family home, condo, or townhome, is typically the most affordable category of real estate legal work, commonly priced as a flat fee in the range of a few hundred to around a thousand dollars. The exact number depends on the purchase price, whether the transaction is a purchase or a sale, and how much complexity the deal is expected to involve.

### Commercial Real Estate

[Commercial real estate](/orland-park/commercial-real-estate-attorney) transactions, whether acquiring an office building, negotiating a retail lease, or purchasing multi-family property, generally cost more than residential work because they involve more extensive due diligence, financing negotiation, and heavily negotiated purchase agreements rather than a standard form contract. Pricing for commercial matters is more often structured hourly or as a larger flat fee scoped to the specific deal.

### Litigation and Contested Matters

When a real estate dispute cannot be resolved through negotiation, whether a contract breach, a boundary dispute, or a title defect that requires a quiet title action, the matter typically shifts to hourly billing. Litigation costs vary enormously depending on how contested the case becomes and how long it takes to resolve, which is why an attorney handling a dispute usually cannot offer a flat quote at the outset with any real accuracy.

## What Affects the Cost of Your Transaction

Even within the flat-fee category, a few factors tend to push pricing up or down.

- **Purchase price and property type.** Higher-value transactions and multi-unit properties often involve more complexity than a straightforward single-family resale.
- **Title complications.** A property with a lien, judgment, or unclear chain of title requires additional attorney work to resolve, which can add to the cost beyond a standard flat fee.
- **Short sales and distressed properties.** [Short sale](/orland-park/short-sale-attorney) transactions require additional communication with lenders and longer timelines, which some firms account for with a different fee structure than a standard sale.
- **New construction contracts.** Builder contracts require more thorough review than a standard resale contract, since the terms are often drafted entirely by the builder's counsel.
- **Whether disputes arise during the transaction.** A deal that proceeds smoothly from contract to closing costs less attorney time than one involving repeated renegotiation, inspection disputes, or a buyer or seller who backs out.

## Why Cheap Isn't Always Better

It can be tempting to choose whichever attorney quotes the lowest number, but price alone does not tell you much about the level of attention your file will receive. A very low flat fee can sometimes mean a high-volume practice where files are handled with less individualized review, slower response times, or additional charges added later for tasks that should have been included from the start.

What matters more than the number itself is what is actually included in the fee: does it cover attorney review negotiation, title review, coordination with your lender, attendance at closing, and reasonable email and phone communication throughout? A clear answer to that question tells you far more about value than the quoted price alone.

## What a Flat Fee Usually Does Not Include

Understanding the boundaries of a standard flat fee is just as important as understanding what it covers. Most residential flat fees are built around a transaction that proceeds in a fairly normal way, from contract to closing, without a contested dispute. If a deal collapses into litigation, such as a fight over earnest money after a failed closing, that work typically falls outside the original flat fee and is billed separately, usually hourly. Similarly, extensive title curative work, such as resolving a decades-old boundary dispute or clearing a lien that requires court involvement, often falls outside standard flat-fee pricing since the time required is difficult to predict in advance.

This is not a hidden cost so much as a natural consequence of how flat fees work: they are priced around the scope of a normal transaction, and matters that go beyond that scope require additional time that a flat fee was never designed to cover. A transparent attorney will explain this distinction before you sign on, so there are no surprises if your transaction turns out to be more complicated than expected.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

Since fee structures vary from firm to firm, it helps to ask a few direct questions before committing to representation. Does the quoted fee include attorney review and negotiation of the contract, or is that billed separately? Is title review included, and what happens if a title problem requires extra work to resolve? Does the fee cover attendance at closing, whether in person or remote? Are phone calls and emails throughout the transaction included, or billed as they occur? A firm that can answer these questions clearly upfront, rather than deferring to "it depends" without explanation, is generally a good sign of how the rest of the representation will go.

It is also worth asking whether the fee changes if the transaction becomes more complicated than expected, such as a buyer's financing falling through and the property going back on the market, or a title defect requiring curative work. Firms that build reasonable flexibility into their flat fee, rather than nickel-and-diming clients for every unexpected turn, tend to provide a much better client experience overall.

It is worth putting attorney fees in context against the rest of a real estate transaction. Real estate commissions, lender fees, title insurance premiums, recording fees, and prorated taxes typically make up a much larger share of your total closing costs than attorney fees do. Given how much financial and legal exposure is at stake in a home purchase or sale, the relatively modest cost of legal representation is one of the better values in the entire transaction, particularly when weighed against the potential cost of an unresolved title problem or a contract dispute after the fact.
