After weeks of contract negotiation, inspections, and lender paperwork, closing day can feel like both the finish line and a bit of a mystery if you have never been through one before. Knowing what to expect ahead of time makes the process far less stressful, whether you are buying your first home or selling a property you have owned for decades. The [real estate closing attorney](/orland-park/real-estate-closing-attorney) team at Tommalieh Law walks clients through this process every week, and this article covers what actually happens from the moment you arrive to the weeks after you leave.

## Before Closing Day Arrives

By the time closing is scheduled, your attorney has typically already reviewed your Closing Disclosure or settlement statement and flagged any concerns. It is worth reading through that document before closing day rather than for the first time at the table, so any questions can be resolved in advance. Your attorney should walk you through the final numbers, including how much money you need to bring if you are the buyer, or what you can expect to net if you are the seller.

Most buyers also complete a final walkthrough of the property in the day or two before closing, confirming the home is in the condition agreed to in the contract and that any negotiated repairs have actually been completed. If something is wrong during the walkthrough, it is much easier to address before closing than after.

## What Documents to Bring

Every closing requires a valid, government-issued photo ID for anyone signing documents. Beyond that, requirements differ depending on whether you are buying or selling.

- **Buyers** typically need a cashier's check or confirmation of a completed wire transfer covering their portion of closing costs and down payment, since personal checks are rarely accepted for the funds due at closing.
- **Sellers** should bring keys, garage door openers, and any warranty or manual paperwork for major systems and appliances staying with the home.
- **Both parties** should bring any documents specifically requested by their attorney, such as proof of homeowners insurance for buyers or payoff information for an existing mortgage for sellers.

Your attorney's office will typically send a checklist ahead of time confirming exactly what you need to bring so there are no surprises at the table.

## Who Is in the Room

A traditional in-person closing usually includes the buyer, the seller, each party's attorney, and a representative from the title company handling the closing. Real estate agents often attend as well, though their role at this stage is mostly supportive since the legal and financial details are handled by the attorneys and title company. Depending on the transaction, a lender representative may also be present, though most lenders now handle their side of the paperwork ahead of time rather than attending in person.

### Remote and Mail-Away Closings

Not every closing happens with all parties physically together. It has become increasingly common, especially when a buyer or seller lives out of state or a schedule cannot be coordinated, to use a mail-away closing. In this arrangement, documents are prepared in advance and either mailed to the signer with a local notary coordinated to witness the signature, or in some cases handled through a power of attorney. Your attorney reviews every document with you by phone or video beforehand so you understand exactly what you are signing, even if you are not sitting across the table from the other party.

## How Long Closing Actually Takes

Once everyone is at the table with documents ready, the actual signing typically takes thirty minutes to an hour for a standard residential transaction. More complex transactions, or those involving multiple lenders, trusts, or estates, can take longer. The broader timeline, from when documents are signed to when funds actually disburse and the deal is considered fully closed, can extend a bit further depending on when the lender wires loan proceeds and when the title company confirms everything has cleared.

For financed purchases, the lender typically wires funds to the title company on or just before closing day. Your attorney confirms those funds have arrived before treating the transaction as complete, since documents are not truly final until the money has actually moved.

## What Happens With Funds

At closing, money moves in several directions at once. The buyer's down payment and closing costs, along with the lender's loan proceeds if applicable, come into the title company's escrow account. From there, funds are disbursed to pay off the seller's existing mortgage, pay real estate commissions, pay any outstanding property taxes or municipal charges, and finally pay the seller their net proceeds. Your attorney reviews this disbursement against the agreed settlement statement to confirm everything matches what was negotiated.

### Escrow Holdbacks

Occasionally, an amount is held back in escrow after closing rather than disbursed immediately, often to cover a repair that could not be completed before closing or a minor title issue still being cleared. Your attorney tracks these holdbacks to make sure funds are released once the underlying condition is satisfied, rather than letting them sit forgotten in an escrow account.

## Getting the Keys

Once the transaction has fully funded, which in most cases happens the same day as closing, the buyer is entitled to possession according to the terms of the contract. Keys, garage remotes, and any access codes are typically handed over at the closing table itself or shortly after funding is confirmed. If the contract includes a different possession date, such as a seller staying in the home for a short period after closing under a temporary lease-back arrangement, that timeline controls instead.

## After Closing: Recording the Deed

Once closing is complete, the deed transferring ownership, along with any new mortgage, still needs to be recorded with the county. Depending on where the property is located, this means filing with the Cook County, Will County, or DuPage County Recorder of Deeds. This step is typically handled by the title company or closing attorney within a few business days of closing, and it is what makes the transfer part of the official public record, protecting the new owner's title against later claims.

Buyers and sellers throughout [Aurora](/aurora/real-estate-lawyer) and the surrounding suburbs sometimes assume the transaction is completely finished the moment they leave the closing table, but a few administrative steps still happen behind the scenes afterward, including final recording and any remaining escrow releases. Your attorney's office typically follows up once recording is confirmed so you have a copy for your records.

## Common Closing Day Surprises and How to Avoid Them

Even a well-prepared closing can include a small surprise or two, and knowing what commonly comes up can help you stay calm if it happens to you. A closing figure might shift slightly at the last minute due to a per diem interest adjustment or a final utility reading. A lender might request one more document the morning of closing. A seller might need a few extra minutes to locate every garage remote or appliance manual promised in the contract. None of these are cause for alarm, and an experienced attorney handles them as a routine part of getting to the table, not as a reason to panic.

The bigger surprises worth watching for are the ones tied to money and title: a wire transfer that has not yet arrived, a lien that was not cleared as expected, or a settlement figure that does not match what you were told earlier in the week. These are exactly the kinds of issues your attorney should be actively checking for before you ever sit down to sign, which is why reviewing your Closing Disclosure or settlement statement in advance, rather than for the first time at the table, matters so much.

## Preparing Financially and Emotionally for Closing Day

Beyond the paperwork, it helps to prepare practically for the day itself. Confirm your wire transfer or cashier's check is ready at least a day in advance, since same-day wires can occasionally face processing delays outside anyone's control. Plan for the closing to take longer than the actual signing time once you account for travel, parking, and any last-minute questions that come up. If you are a seller, plan your move-out timeline around the possession date in your contract rather than assuming you can stay past closing without an agreement in place.

For many buyers and sellers, closing day carries real emotional weight on top of the logistics, whether it is the excitement of a first home or the bittersweet feeling of leaving one you have lived in for years. Having an attorney who has walked dozens of clients through this exact process can make the day feel far more manageable, since you are not figuring out what is normal and what is not for the first time on your own.
