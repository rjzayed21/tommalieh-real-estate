Selling a home involves more legal exposure than most sellers expect, from the disclosures you sign at listing to the liability you can carry for years after the deed is recorded. While Illinois does not require sellers to hire an attorney, the vast majority do, and for good reason. The team at [Tommalieh Law](/orland-park/residential-real-estate-lawyer) represents sellers throughout the southern Chicago suburbs, and this article walks through what a seller's attorney actually handles and why skipping that representation can be a costly shortcut.

## The Seller's Side of Attorney Review

Just as buyers have a window to have their attorney review the purchase contract, sellers benefit from the same attorney review period, typically five business days from contract acceptance under the standard Illinois form contract. During that window, your attorney can push back on unfavorable terms your agent may not have caught, tighten deadlines, and clarify what is and is not included in the sale, from window treatments to appliances.

Sellers sometimes assume that because they drafted or accepted the listing terms, the contract is already final once signed. In practice, the buyer's attorney is very likely to send a review letter proposing changes, and having your own attorney respond protects you from simply accepting whatever the other side requests. This back-and-forth is a normal part of nearly every transaction, not a sign that something has gone wrong.

### Listing Agreement vs. the Purchase Contract

It is worth understanding the difference between two separate documents: the listing agreement you sign with your real estate agent, which governs commission and marketing terms, and the purchase contract you sign with the buyer once an offer is accepted. An attorney's review typically focuses on the purchase contract, since that is the document that creates binding obligations between you and the buyer, including deadlines, contingencies, and what happens if either side fails to perform.

## Disclosure Obligations Under Illinois Law

Illinois law generally requires sellers of most residential real estate to complete a written disclosure report identifying known material defects in the property before the buyer signs the contract. This covers things like a leaking roof, a wet basement, prior flooding, defective electrical wiring, and known problems with major systems. The obligation is based on what the seller actually knows, not on defects a professional inspection might later uncover, but getting the disclosure form right matters enormously.

An attorney can help you complete this disclosure accurately, which protects you two ways. First, an honest and complete disclosure reduces the chance a buyer can later claim you concealed a known problem. Second, careful phrasing prevents you from disclosing more than the law requires or creating ambiguity that a buyer's attorney could use to renegotiate price or credits during attorney review.

### Avoiding Liability After Closing

One of the most important reasons to have an attorney involved before you sign disclosure paperwork is what happens after closing. If a buyer later discovers a defect and believes you knew about it and failed to disclose it, they may have grounds to bring a claim against you, sometimes years down the road. Sellers who work with counsel to prepare accurate, complete disclosures at the outset are in a far stronger position to defend themselves if a dispute like this ever arises. If a dispute does develop, our [real estate litigation](/orland-park/real-estate-litigation-attorney) team can also step in to defend a seller against a post-closing claim.

## Negotiating Repairs and Credits After Inspection

Once a buyer completes their home inspection, it is common for them to come back with a list of requested repairs or a request for a credit at closing to cover the cost of fixing issues themselves. This negotiation happens within the deadlines set by the contract, and how it is handled can significantly affect your net proceeds from the sale.

A seller's attorney reviews the inspection request, advises which items are reasonable to address and which fall outside what the contract requires, and drafts the response. Some issues, like a missing smoke detector or a minor cosmetic flaw, are not worth fighting over. Others, like a furnace nearing the end of its life or evidence of past water intrusion, can lead to substantial credit requests that deserve careful negotiation rather than an automatic yes.

### When Title Issues Surface During a Sale

It is also common for title problems to surface once a title search is run on the property you are selling, particularly if you have owned the home for many years. Old liens that were never released, unpaid special assessments, or boundary discrepancies revealed by a new survey can all delay or derail a closing. Our [title issues attorney](/orland-park/title-issues-attorney) team resolves these problems regularly, working with the title company and any lienholders to get a clean title ready in time for closing.

## Preparing Your Title Before You List

Many sellers do not think about title until a buyer's attorney or title company raises an issue mid-transaction, but problems are often much easier to address before the home is even listed. If you inherited the property, went through a divorce, refinanced multiple times, or added a name to the deed years ago, it is worth having an attorney pull a preliminary title report early. Common issues we find include an old mortgage that was paid off but never formally released, a mechanic's lien from a contractor dispute long forgotten, or a deed that was never properly recorded after a family transfer.

Discovering and resolving these issues before you accept an offer means you are negotiating from a position of strength rather than scrambling to clear title while a buyer's financing deadline is ticking down. It also reduces the chance that a title problem forces you to delay or renegotiate a closing date after you have already made plans to move.

### Multiple Offers and Escalation Terms

In a competitive market, sellers sometimes receive several offers at once, some with escalation clauses, waived contingencies, or unusual financing terms. An attorney can help you compare these offers on more than just price, looking at how strong the financing actually is, how many contingencies remain, and how quickly the buyer is prepared to close. The most attractive number on paper is not always the offer most likely to actually reach the closing table.

## What Happens if a Buyer Backs Out

Sellers sometimes assume that once a contract is signed, the sale is guaranteed to happen. In reality, most standard contracts give buyers several ways to exit, including financing contingencies, inspection contingencies, and the attorney review period itself. If a buyer terminates during one of these windows, the seller is usually back on the market without much recourse beyond returning the earnest money.

Where a buyer breaches the contract outside of a valid contingency, however, sellers may have options, including retaining the earnest money as liquidated damages or, in some cases, pursuing further legal action. An attorney can advise which path makes sense given your specific contract language and circumstances.

## Working With Out-of-State or Inherited Property

Not every seller lives in the home they are selling. We regularly work with clients who inherited a property from a family member, relocated for work before selling their prior home, or are selling a rental property from out of state. These situations often add extra layers of complexity, from confirming an estate has legal authority to sell, to coordinating a mail-away closing so the seller never has to travel back to Illinois, to making sure property taxes and any tenant leases are properly accounted for in the sale.

An attorney handling these details early prevents last-minute scrambling once a buyer is under contract and deadlines are already running. If the property came through an estate, it is especially important to confirm probate has been completed or that the executor has clear legal authority to convey title, since a title company will not close without documentation confirming that authority is in place.

On closing day itself, your attorney reviews your final settlement figures, confirms the payoff of any existing mortgage, coordinates the payment of outstanding property taxes or special assessments, and makes sure the deed and transfer documents are prepared correctly for recording with the county.
