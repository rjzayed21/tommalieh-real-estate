By the time closing day arrives, most of the heavy lifting in a real estate transaction has already happened, but the attorney's job is far from over. Closing is where months of contract negotiation, title work, and lender coordination all come together in a single sitting, and having an experienced [real estate closing attorney](/orland-park/real-estate-closing-attorney) in your corner that day is what keeps small last-minute issues from turning into major delays. This article walks through exactly what a real estate attorney does on closing day, from the final numbers to the moment the deed gets recorded.

## Reviewing the Closing Disclosure and Settlement Statement

In the days leading up to closing, your attorney receives the Closing Disclosure from the lender, which outlines the final loan terms, closing costs, and cash needed to close. For transactions without a mortgage, or to reconcile the broader picture of funds moving between all parties, a settlement statement is also prepared showing every credit and debit affecting buyer and seller.

Your attorney compares these documents line by line against the purchase contract and any negotiated repair credits or seller concessions. Errors here are more common than buyers and sellers expect, whether it is a prorated tax figure that does not match the agreed formula, a missing credit, or a fee that should not be there at all. Catching these discrepancies before you sit down at the closing table, rather than after money has already changed hands, is one of the most valuable things your attorney does.

### Confirming Prorations and Credits

Property taxes in Illinois are paid in arrears, meaning the tax bill you receive covers the prior year. As a result, nearly every closing includes a proration calculation dividing responsibility for taxes between buyer and seller based on how long each owned the property during the tax year. Your attorney checks that this calculation, along with any negotiated repair credits or seller-paid closing costs, is reflected accurately in the final numbers.

## Resolving Last-Minute Title Issues

Even after a title commitment has been issued and reviewed earlier in the transaction, new issues can surface close to closing. A judgment might be entered against a prior owner, a final water bill might be discovered unpaid, or a lien release that was supposed to be recorded might still be pending. Your attorney works directly with the title company to resolve these problems, which may involve obtaining payoff letters, lien releases, or affidavits so that a clean [title insurance](/orland-park/title-issues-attorney) policy can be issued to the buyer and lender at closing.

This is also the point where your attorney confirms that any existing mortgage on the property being sold will be paid off and released, and that the new deed correctly reflects how title is being taken, whether as sole ownership, joint tenancy, or tenancy in common. Getting this detail wrong can create real complications for a future sale or estate matter down the road.

### Coordinating With the Lender

For financed purchases, your attorney also coordinates with the buyer's mortgage lender to confirm the loan is cleared to fund and that all conditions from underwriting have been satisfied. Any last-minute lender request, whether it is an updated pay stub or a final verification of employment, needs to be resolved before the closing can proceed, and your attorney helps keep that communication moving so the closing stays on schedule.

## Attending Closing, In Person or Remote

Closings in the Chicago area can take a few different forms depending on the title company and the parties involved. In many cases, buyer, seller, and both attorneys sit down together, often at a title company office, to sign the final documents. In other cases, particularly when a party lives out of state or a schedule conflict arises, a mail-away closing is arranged where documents are signed in advance and reviewed remotely by the attorney, or a power of attorney is used to complete signing on someone's behalf.

Whichever format is used, your attorney's role is the same: confirming that every document you are signing matches what was negotiated, explaining anything unclear before you sign, and making sure funds are only released once every condition of closing has been satisfied. ### The Final Walkthrough

Most buyers complete a final walkthrough of the property shortly before closing to confirm it is in the condition agreed to in the contract and that any negotiated repairs were completed. If a problem turns up during that walkthrough, your attorney can address it before closing proceeds, whether by delaying signing briefly, negotiating a credit, or in rare cases postponing the closing until the issue is resolved.

## Common Closing Day Issues and How They Get Resolved

Even well-prepared transactions can run into a hiccup on closing day, and how your attorney handles it often determines whether closing still happens on schedule. A few examples come up often enough to be worth understanding in advance.

- **A wire transfer delay.** If a buyer's down payment wire is delayed by their bank, your attorney communicates with the title company about realistic timing rather than letting the closing collapse over a timing issue that resolves within hours.
- **A last-minute lien or judgment.** A search update sometimes reveals a new lien filed against the seller between the original title commitment and closing. Your attorney works to obtain a payoff or release, or arranges for funds to be escrowed until it is cleared.
- **A walkthrough discrepancy.** If the final walkthrough reveals a repair was not completed as agreed, or an item that was supposed to remain with the home is missing, your attorney can negotiate a credit or a short delay rather than letting the entire closing fall apart.
- **A name or entity discrepancy.** If the buyer is taking title in the name of a trust or an LLC that was not reflected correctly in earlier paperwork, your attorney coordinates with the title company to correct the documents before signing.

None of these issues are unusual, and none of them typically derail a closing when an experienced attorney is handling the file. They become serious problems primarily when no one catches them until it is too late to fix.

## Handling Funds and Disbursement

Once documents are signed, the title company or escrow agent disburses funds according to the settlement statement. This includes paying off the seller's existing mortgage, paying real estate commissions, paying the seller their net proceeds, and paying any other closing costs owed. Your attorney monitors this process to confirm the numbers match what was reviewed and approved beforehand, and addresses any last-minute discrepancy immediately rather than after the fact.

For buyers using a mortgage, the lender typically wires loan funds to the title company shortly before or on the day of closing, and your attorney confirms those funds have arrived and the transaction is cleared to fund before signing is treated as final.

## Recording the Deed

After closing, the deed transferring ownership, along with the new mortgage if one exists, is submitted to the county Recorder of Deeds, whether that is Cook, Will, or DuPage County depending on where the property sits. Recording makes the transfer part of the public record and protects the buyer's ownership interest against later claims. This typically happens within a few business days of closing, and your attorney or the title company confirms the recording has gone through and that a recorded copy is eventually provided to the new owner.

### Post-Closing Follow-Up

Even after keys are handed over, a few items often need attention. This can include making sure any escrow holdback for a repair is released once the work is completed, confirming a final water bill or homeowners association transfer fee has been paid, or resolving a title curative item that was cleared with an escrow arrangement rather than before closing. A good attorney does not disappear the moment documents are signed; they follow the file through to make sure every loose end is actually tied off.
